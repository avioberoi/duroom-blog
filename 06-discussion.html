Discussion

Can the works a culture has already made tell us where it has not been, when all we measure is how far a candidate sits from them? Our answer is in the Introduction. Here we ask what to make of what we found (Results overview).

Do later works sit far from the works before 1900?

More often than earlier works do. The Data chapter shows it before any picture is painted. A basis from the works before 1900 keeps about 9 points less of the later works than a random basis does (Q2). From 1900 to the 1960s each decade leaned further in one direction, and then held there. The decades moved first where Cubism and abstraction sit, then where Minimalism and installation sit (Q3). Most works from 1960 on sit where earlier works are almost absent, and a score built only from the works before 1900 ranks a later work above an earlier one about 90% of the time in DINOv3 and SigLIP (Q6). No decoder and no search is involved in any of this.

But far alone does not lead to later works. Most later works stay under the bar, noise is far too, and with no decoder, following the score in CLIP from an earlier work does not lead toward later works (Q12). So far tells us where the works before 1900 are sparse. It does not tell us what sits there (Q7).

How does our search find far and faithful pictures?

It paints and measures, and keeps to pictures (i.e., restarts from what the decoder did paint). The works give a search no reliable rule for where a far picture will come from (Q1) and no formula for where art went next (Q9). But a straight walk from the older works toward the 1960s never runs empty of works (Q5), and the decoder follows a moved vector part of the way (Q8). So we can paint a place and measure where the picture lands. The free search, left to follow distance alone, goes far and brings back nothing far and faithful. DuROOM search restarts each round from its own pictures and keeps only faithful ones (Q12).

A good part of the work is done without the score. The score makes it happen more often. The search’s 2 controls are the same loop told random scores, one with its step and one without. They still paint far and faithful pictures about a third and three fifths as often as the search. We read the loop told the real score as a ratchet on the decoder’s noise.1 It paints far and faithful pictures nearly 3 times as often as with random scores (Q12). Most of its far and faithful pictures sit where art went only after 1900, i.e., a work dated 1900 or later is within the manifold line of them and none dated before.2 Of those that the art before 1900 also describes badly, it paints 19 times as many as with random scores.

Why do different starts arrive at the same works?

On the manifold, pictures from different starts often arrive at the same later works, and each start also keeps works of its own. Off it we found no place where they meet. From 5 unrelated works the search took most of its pictures to the same later works, stripe painting and op art. At equal size, 62% of the search’s pictures sit at works reached from 3 or more starts, against 37% of the random-score loop’s (Q11, Q12). Off the manifold, pictures from different starts lie apart. The same test (do pictures from 3 or more starts lie close together?) finds no such place among real works from 1970 on that are far from the art before 1970, though it finds places among those that are not far (appendix). Far real works lie apart from each other too, so pictures that lie apart off the manifold do not by themselves show that the search missed a shared place.

Discovery or recollection?

The same decoder in every comparison lets us measure what the search adds, but not whether its pictures come from the works or from what the decoder learned. The Data chapter’s evidence uses no decoder, so the decoder shows only in where the pictures land. Most of the search’s far and faithful pictures sit where art went only after 1900. So do most of the controls’ pictures, and most far and faithful real later works painted through the decoder. Of the far and faithful pictures the decoder paints from noise, all but one sit there too (Q11, Q12). So landing there is not what the search adds, and it can be a recollection: neither the decoder nor the encoders were limited to images from before 1900.3

Off the manifold of all the art we have, the search paints more, and we cannot tell what that means. Scored against all the art we have, the search paints faithful pictures off the manifold over 10 times as often as its controls (Q11). Off the manifold says only that no work of our corpus sits within the manifold line of a picture. A new kind of picture could sit within it, and an ordinary one that WikiArt lacks could sit outside it.

To our eye, the search’s pictures read as a picture of something about as often as the others do. Off the manifold they look less like the art we have.4

Where does this sit beside earlier work?

Our check against a known future (freeze the corpus at 1900, then look at what came after) is the one used in studies of the new: a song or a paper is scored against a past, and the score is checked against what came later (Askin and Mauskapf 2017; Shi and Evans 2023; Uzzi et al. 2013). A smooth trend in a curated corpus can come from the curators’ selection (Lee et al. 2020), and a corpus read through a model is also read through its training data (Impett and Offert 2022). Both hold for us too. Novelty alone can go anywhere, as an agent rewarded for what it cannot predict gets stuck at a noisy TV (Burda et al. 2019), and our free search is much like that case. A search for artificial-life simulations scored in a foundation model’s space found known forms such as flocking again (Kumar et al. 2025), and ours mostly lands among art that exists too, the art dated 1900 or later (Q11).

Strengths and limits

The corpus is frozen at 1900 and the bar is built only from works before it, so the later works are a future the bar never saw, though they helped choose the score (Q6). The search is set against 2 controls with the same decoder and budget, from the same 5 starts (Q12).

We have one corpus and one decoder. Every rate here rests on one run per start, and runs move (Q12). We chose the pictures shown by eye, or drew them at random.

Our tests do not settle whether the works also show where art could go next, as they measure what the search adds with this decoder. The works made after 1900 are one future among those the past allowed. A search could be right about a future that did not happen, and we could not tell.

Future directions

Next we would train a decoder only on works before 1900 and repaint the works the bar is set on, and the real later works, with it (Q12). A blind rating of the pictures by other viewers is planned, subject to IRB review.

If a decoder that has only seen the past also drifts toward the later works when it repaints its own pictures, some of where art went was already in where it had been. If it does not, what we watched may be memory, or that decoder may paint too poorly to tell. Neither answer would be clean, as CLIP and the 2 observers were not limited to images from before 1900 either. And maybe that is the question we should have started with, i.e., when a system frozen in the past finds something far from it, how would we tell a discovery from a recollection, without a future to check it against?

Discussion

Can the works a culture has already made tell us where it has not been, when all we measure is how far a candidate sits from them? Our answer is in the Introduction. Here we ask what to make of what we found (Results overview).

Do later works sit far from the works before 1900?

More often than earlier works do. The Data chapter shows it before any picture is painted. In CLIP, a basis built from the works before 1900 keeps less of the later works than a random basis does (Q2), each decade from 1900 to the 1960s leaned further in one direction (first where Cubism and abstraction sit, then where Minimalism and installation sit) and then held (Q3), and most works from 1960 on sit where earlier works are almost absent. In DINOv3 and SigLIP, a score built only from the works before 1900 ranks a later work above an earlier one most of the time (Q6).1

But far alone does not lead to later works. Most later works stay under the bar, noise is far too, and with no decoder, following the score in CLIP from an earlier work does not lead toward later works (Q12). So far tells us where the works before 1900 are sparse. It does not tell us what sits there (Q7).

How does our search find far and faithful pictures?

It paints and measures, and keeps to pictures (i.e., restarts from what the decoder did paint). The works give a search no reliable rule for where a far picture will come from (Q1) and no formula for where art went next (Q9). But a straight walk from the older works toward the 1960s never runs empty of works in CLIP (Q5), and the decoder follows a moved vector part of the way (Q8). So we can paint a place and measure where the picture lands. The search moves in CLIP, as the decoder paints from a CLIP vector. Far is read on the painted picture, as moving a vector changes its CLIP distance whether or not the picture changes. It is read by 2 encoders the search does not move in (Q7).

One old answer to how new things arise is that variations are made without knowing which will succeed, and some are selected and kept (Campbell 1960). DuROOM search is that answer run on pictures. The decoder and the step vary the pictures, the faithful check and the score select, and the restart keeps each round at pictures the decoder did paint. Taking away the score, and then the step, shows what those 2 are for (Q12).2

Selection by the score multiplies the far and faithful pictures. The works give a search no single map of directions to steer by (Q1), and a place has to be painted before we know how far it is (Q5). So the search cannot aim before it paints. It varies at random around its centre, and the score chooses which of its pictures the next round starts from. With the real score in place of random ones, the same loop paints nearly 3 times as many.3

The step adds little to the number of far pictures. Under random scores, what it adds is far pictures that lie apart. The decoder varies its pictures even when every request is the same. Told the real score, the loop with no step nearly keeps up with the search.4

What a loop keeps matters. Kept to pictures the decoder did paint, the search and its 2 controls all paint far and faithful ones. They stay faithful even with no step and no real score, and the loop with neither paints more than half as many as the search. The free search (left to follow distance alone) keeps its requests and not its pictures, with larger steps and no faithful check, and none of its far pictures is faithful.5

Selecting by far in CLIP, in place of the 2 observers, leaves the number of far and faithful pictures about the same, and selection still multiplies them over random scores.6

Why do different starts arrive at the same works?

In all 3 loops, neither the start nor the score sets which region a loop ends in. Every run of the search and its 2 controls, from all 5 starts, ends in the same region of CLIP, a region where three quarters of the works are from 1960 on.7 Loops started from Cossiers’s Martyrdom of St Catherine of 1641 and from Sisley’s riverbank of 1885 end in the same region, with the score or without it. And in every loop, most far and faithful pictures sit where art went only after 1900 (a work dated 1900 or later is within the manifold line of them, and none dated before).8 The Data chapter fits both: in CLIP the way from the older works toward the 1960s never runs empty of works (Q5), and most works from 1960 on sit where earlier works are almost absent (Q6).9

Where the old works are scarce, selection adds 2 things: more of what is painted is far and faithful, and the starts meet at the same works more often.10 Ending in the same region does not bring the starts to the same works: the loop told random scores is there as long as the search, and its starts share works less often.

On the manifold, pictures from different starts often arrive at the same later works, and each start also keeps works of its own. Off it we found no place where they meet. From 5 starts the search took most of its far and faithful pictures to the same later works, stripe painting and op art (Q11). Off the manifold, pictures from different starts lie apart, and the search paints them in a few stretches of rounds, earlier than its other far and faithful pictures.11 Far real works lie apart from each other too: the same rule (do pictures from 3 or more starts lie close together?), with random groups of artists in place of the starts, finds no such place among real works from 1970 on that are far from the art before 1970, though it finds places among those that are not far (appendix). So pictures that lie apart off the manifold do not by themselves show that the search missed a shared place.

Every loop’s pictures lean the way the later works lean, but that does not say the loops went where art went. Pictures painted from requests read from noise images lean that way as much as repainted works of 1900 to 1969, and pictures painted from requests built from the old works do not.12

What part does the decoder play?

A picture counts as far and faithful only when it reads back within the faithful line of its request (Q7). The search and its controls all use the same decoder, so they differ in the loop and not in the decoder. And on one repaint of a real work’s own vector, old stays old: of 1,000 works from before 1900, each repainted from its own vector, 99% are near the works before 1900 and faithful (appendix). A second decoder, unCLIP, repaints a work about equally near it (on 256 works, Q8). The far and faithful later works, repainted, mostly sit where art went only after 1900 too, as do all but one of the far and faithful pictures painted from requests read from noise images.13

Scored against all the art we have, the search paints faithful pictures off the manifold over 10 times as often as its controls in sum (Q11). Off the manifold says only that no work of our corpus sits within the manifold line of a picture in either observer.14

To our eye, the search’s pictures read as a picture of something about as often as the others do. Off the manifold they look less like the art we have.15

Where does this sit beside earlier work?

Our check against a known future (freeze the corpus at 1900, then look at what came after) is the one used in studies of the new: a song or a paper is scored against a past, and the score is checked against what came later (Askin and Mauskapf 2017; Shi and Evans 2023; Uzzi et al. 2013). A smooth trend in a curated corpus can come from the curators’ selection (Lee et al. 2020), and ours is curated too. Novelty alone can go anywhere, as an agent rewarded for what it cannot predict gets stuck at a noisy TV (Burda et al. 2019), and our free search is much like that case. Faithful, with the restart, played the part of the minimal criterion of novelty search (Lehman and Stanley 2010): the free search, with neither, goes far and brings back nothing far and faithful, and DuROOM search, with both, finds far pictures the decoder did paint.16 A search for artificial-life simulations scored in a foundation model’s space found known forms such as flocking again (Kumar et al. 2025), and ours mostly lands among art that exists too, the art dated 1900 or later (Q11).

Strengths and limits

The corpus is frozen at 1900 and the bar is built only from works before it, so the later works are a future the bar never saw, though they helped choose the score (Q6). The search is set against 2 controls with the same decoder and budget, from the same 5 starts (Q12). Far is scored in DINOv3 and SigLIP. With far read in CLIP instead (where the search moves), the search has more pictures far in CLIP and faithful than the random-score loop at every start and than the no-step loop at 4 of the 5, as with the observers (appendix). With far read in AIMv2, the one encoder outside the loop, it has 13% and 16% more than its 2 controls, and more at 4 of the 5 starts against each (appendix).17

We have one corpus, and every loop ran on one decoder. Every rate here rests on one run per start, and runs move (Q12). We chose the pictures shown by eye, or drew them at random.

The works made after 1900 are one future among those the past allowed. A search could be right about a future that did not happen, and we could not tell.

Future directions

Next we would train a decoder only on works before 1900 and repaint the works the bar is set on, and the real later works, with it (Q12). A blind rating of the pictures by other viewers is planned, subject to IRB review.
